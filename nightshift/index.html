NightShift

A Doctor Who inspired game

A first person arcade horror game where statues hunt the player through a graveyard maze, only moving when unobserved. Collect three keys and escape a rain-soaked graveyard while keeping the statues in sight.

Project overview

How NightShift started

Around June 2024, I was rewatching the Matt Smith era of Doctor Who and got to the Weeping Angels episodes. I've always liked the idea of them. They're statues that can only move when you're not looking, which sounds like it should be terrifying, but watching the episodes again I found that they weren't scaring me the way they used to. I kept thinking about why, because the concept still felt like such a good one.

I started comparing them to The Silence, another villain from the show. With The Silence, you forget that you've seen them the moment you look away. That works well on television because we, as the audience, remember what the characters have forgotten. So we're watching someone walk straight back into a situation that we know is dangerous, while they have no idea. I couldn't see a way to translate that into a game where the player would still remember everything they'd seen.

The Angels were interesting for the opposite reason. If I put them in a game, the player would be the one deciding where to look. You'd have to turn away eventually, whether it was to check where you were going or see what was behind you, and you'd know what that meant for the statue you'd just stopped watching. I wanted to see what that felt like. Would the same idea scare me more if I was the one holding the controller?

So I opened Unreal and started trying to build it. I'd been teaching myself Blueprints, partly because I wanted to eventually make the kinds of open-world games I spend so much time playing. Visual scripting made it feel approachable, and the engine being free helped too. There was also a lot of excitement around UE5, Nanite and Lumen at the time, and I got caught up in all of that even though I had very little use for those things in this particular experiment.

The funny part is that I don't play many horror games. This started because I was curious about one interaction and wanted to experience it for myself. The more I worked on it, the more I wanted to figure out what I could build around it, and that's where the project started getting away from me.

Gameplay prototyping

Testing the statue mechanic

I had some experience with Blueprints from following a 3D platformer tutorial, so I started with what I knew and tried to get a test working as quickly as I could. The scene was a gray floor, a few cubes to give me something to move around and a first-person camera. I used a Hebe statue from a free museum environment pack, and the player controller came from Horror Engine, which already had things like a flashlight and head-bobbing set up.

At the time, having all of that ready seemed like a good way to save myself some work. I could drop the statues into a scene and focus on getting them to move. I didn't give much thought to how many other features came with Horror Engine or what it would take to remove them later. That became a problem much closer to release.

The first version of the statue logic was basic. I used a render check in Blueprints to see whether the statue was on screen. If it wasn't, it would turn towards the player and move forward. Looking at it would stop it. There was no pathfinding, so putting anything in its way was enough to get it stuck, but on an empty floor it did what I needed for the test.

And it creeped me out. I knew what was going to happen because I'd just set it up, but there was still something uncomfortable about looking away for a second and turning back to find the statue closer. That was the feeling I'd been curious about while watching Doctor Who, and now I wanted to see if I could get other people to feel it too.

Of course, I still needed to figure out what the player would be doing while this was happening. Standing on a gray floor and looking back and forth at a statue would only stay interesting for so long. I needed somewhere to go, a reason to go there and a way for the statue to follow me without getting stuck on the first wall. I started thinking about the setting before I'd worked out most of those things.

Scope management

Why I scrapped the museum

My first idea was a museum where you played as a security guard on their first night shift. That seemed like a natural place for statues, and I thought having a story would give people a reason to keep playing. Then I started building on that idea. The museum would have six wings, there would be a time loop, and every time you tried to escape the world would reset. You'd find notes left by previous versions of yourself and slowly piece together what was happening.

I liked the idea enough that I kept trying to make it work, even though I hadn't figured out the basic game underneath it. I was designing this huge space while the statues were still struggling to move around. Every new part of the museum gave me more things to think about, and I was already having trouble deciding where to start.

I tried sketching layouts. While I was on vacation, I even drew a top-down plan in the sand at a kids' playground, because apparently I couldn't leave the museum alone for that long. I also tried building it in Minecraft to see if that would help me work out the space, but I'd get overwhelmed and stop there too.

The project stayed like that for months. I'd come back to it, try to improve something, get stuck and leave it again. Adding line traces to the observation logic was one of those things I had trouble with. There was still no proper level, no progression and no clear sense of how all the pieces would turn into something someone could play through.

I kept working on smaller things in between. I added the scraping sound for the statues, gave them glowing eyes, made some music and worked out what would happen when one caught you. The heartbeat system came out of this period too. Those were all parts I was excited about, and I could see the experience starting to take shape when I tested them together. Then I'd come back to the museum and get stuck trying to fit everything into it again.

Level design

Building the graveyard maze

By mid-October, I was still trying to get the museum to work. Then I had this thought: what if I laid the game out like Pac-Man?

In Pac-Man, you're moving through a maze, collecting pellets and avoiding ghosts. I already had enemies that followed the player, and I needed something for the player to do while avoiding them. Collecting keys through a maze made sense, and it gave me a layout I could understand without having to design an entire museum around it.

Once I started thinking about it that way, a graveyard felt like a much better setting. It could have paths, walls and statues, and it already suited the kind of atmosphere I was going for. I had a graveyard asset pack available, so I put the museum aside and started assembling a level with that. After spending months getting nowhere with the museum, I had a playable environment put together in a single night.

The goal became collecting three keys and escaping the graveyard. That gave me something straightforward to build the rest of the game around. The player would have to move through the map to find the keys, which meant turning corners, checking different paths and looking away from the statues. I could start thinking about where those decisions would happen instead of getting lost in how many rooms a museum should have.

How I laid out the graveyard

I used the Pac-Man grid as the starting point for the four quadrants of the graveyard, building up the walls and enclosing the outside with metal fencing. At each point where a pellet would have been, I placed a footpath block. Those became the references for where the keys could spawn, so the collectibles would be spread along routes the player could walk through.

The map was symmetrical, which helped give me a structure to work with, but I wanted the different parts of it to feel distinct once you were down there in first person. I used trees, walls, shrines and gravestone structures to change what you could see from each path. Some stretches were open enough to see a statue coming from a distance, while others had tighter corners and choke points where you had to turn before you could see what was ahead.

The player starts in the centre beside the largest tree on the map and a group of three shrines. The tree is visible from around the graveyard, so it gives you something to orient yourself by. I liked having that reference within the environment, especially since I wasn't using a map or compass.

The keys have beams of light going up into the sky for a similar reason. You can look for a beam and use it to work out where to go, but doing that also means taking your attention away from whatever statue you were watching. Even finding the objective gives you a reason to look somewhere else.

Moving that layout into first person changed how I had to think about it. From above, I could see the paths and understand the whole maze at once. At ground level, a wall or a tree could hide the route I was looking for. I spent time on those sightlines because so much of this particular game depends on what the player can see, and on what they have to give up seeing when they turn.

Environment design

Set dressing, lighting and weather

This was my first project where I spent dedicated time on environment design, and it made me want to do more of it. Once I had the graveyard laid out, I could start working through the details of how it should feel to walk around in it.

I was using existing assets for the graveyard, so my work was in how I arranged and combined them. Having the paths in place helped because I could build around somewhere the player would be moving through. I could think about what they'd see from a path, what would draw their attention and what would sit further away in the background.

Graves, shrines and trees

I used the Foliage tool to scatter trees around the perimeter and fill the ground with grass. I also added a distant tree line around the map so there was something beyond the fence. I wanted the graveyard to feel like it was sitting within a larger space, even though the player would stay inside it.

Along the inner walls, I placed rows of different gravestones. Some of the graves have disturbed earth, an open wooden coffin and a shovel left nearby, and those are also where the statues spawn. I liked the suggestion that someone had been digging where they shouldn't have and let something out. There's no text explaining that story, but the pieces are there for someone to notice.

I placed a large ruined structure towards the top middle of the map, and smaller shrines around the paths, each with a bust and candles left as a tribute. One of those shrines is broken down into rubble. Since I was starting from such a symmetrical layout, little differences like that helped keep the surroundings from looking the same everywhere.

Lighting and weather

A lot of the lighting came from candles and lanterns placed around the graveyard. I used the Foliage tool to place candles along the floors, walls and graves, then placed the lanterns by hand. Some are on the ground, some sit on lampposts, and one hangs from a tree outside the fence that reaches over into the graveyard.

I used UltraDynamicSky for the weather and spent a long time adjusting it. There was the overall lighting to work out, then the rain, the timing and intensity of the lightning, the debris and the direction of the wind. I kept coming back to how those things felt together while moving through the level. The rain and occasional thunder became a big part of the experience, alongside the music and the sound of the statues moving somewhere around you.

AI implementation

Statue navigation and observation checks

Once the graveyard was in place, I had to get the statues to navigate it. The movement from the first prototype was never going to work through that many walls and corners, so this pushed me to figure out the NavMesh and use MoveToPlayer nodes. After spending so long watching the statues get stuck on things, seeing them find their way through the maze made a big difference.

In the empty test scene, a statue could turn towards me and move straight ahead. In the graveyard, it might have to follow a path around several corners before it could reach me. The NavMesh gave it a way through that space, so I could use the walls and paths in the level without every obstacle stopping the chase. They started feeling like something that could follow me through the graveyard, which was what I needed before I could judge how the game played.

Checking whether a statue is being observed

The released game uses both a render check and a line trace to check whether a statue is being observed. The original test only used the render check, and adding the line trace was one of the things I had to work through as the project developed.

That distinction matters when looking at the early prototype beside the finished level. On the gray floor there was very little between the camera and a statue. In the graveyard, the walls and environment were part of the problem, so I needed to account for the view being blocked as well.

Making the threat easier to read

I also started removing things that no longer made sense in the outdoor level. The flashlight had come with Horror Engine, but it felt like clutter in the graveyard, so I took it out.

Playtesting brought up a different problem. People couldn't tell where the statues were coming from, and getting caught that way was frustrating. I'd spent a lot of time making the statues work, but the people playing still needed a way to understand what was happening around them.

I followed a tutorial for a Call of Duty-style directional indicator and set it up to respond to the nearest statue's position and proximity. That gave the player a visual reference for the threat while keeping the HUD fairly minimal. The heartbeat and scraping sounds were already doing some of that work through audio, and the indicator gave people another way to read the situation.

Sound design and music

Movement sounds, music and the capture sequence

A lot of the audio and feedback came together while I was still stuck on the museum. They were smaller parts I could keep working on while I tried to figure out the rest of the game. I gave the statues a stone scraping sound when they moved, so you could hear that movement even while looking somewhere else. I also gave them red eyes that glowed when the player was nearby. That helped indicate proximity and gave them a slightly different look from the Weeping Angels that had inspired them.

Composing the background music

While I was on vacation, I was messing around on an old keyboard and came across a chord progression I liked: D#7, B6, C#7, F7. It had this unsettling feel to it that seemed like it would fit the game, so I recorded it on my phone.

The recording quality was poor, but I still wanted to use the progression. I recreated it as MIDI in BandLab and made it into a loop, which became the background music for NightShift. I liked being able to make that part myself too, especially once I could hear it in the graveyard with the rain and the other sounds I'd been adding.

What happens when a statue catches you

I needed to work out what happened once a statue reached you. I ended up with a sequence where the player's controls lock, the camera shakes and slowly turns to face the statue, then the screen fades to black with a glitch effect before you respawn.

For the sound, I used a sharp tritone chord, going for that kind of Hitchcock-style stinger. I was pleased with how the sound and camera movement came together. It gave me a small cinematic moment within a game where most of the time you're free to move and look wherever you want.

Procedural audio

Building the heartbeat system

The heartbeat was one of the parts I spent the most time figuring out. I wanted it to speed up as a statue got closer, but I also wanted that distance to make sense within the level. In a maze, something can be close to you on the other side of a wall and still have a long way to travel before it reaches you.

So I used Unreal's navigation system to get the pathing distance between the player and the nearest statue. That accounted for the obstacles along the route, and I used that distance to adjust how fast the heartbeat played. As the statue got closer along its path, the heartbeat would get faster.

That gave the player information about a statue they might not be able to see. You could be moving through the graveyard and hear the heartbeat picking up, knowing something was getting closer while you were still trying to find the keys. I liked how it added to the panic without needing another thing on the screen.

I built the rhythm out of separate “lub” and “dub” sounds and adjusted the delays between them. That meant I had to manage the individual sounds as the pace changed, including making sure they didn't start overlapping. Getting all of that to work together took some figuring out, because I was dealing with both the distance calculation and the timing of the audio.

This became my first procedural audio system. Hearing it respond while I moved around the level was satisfying, especially because it tied together things I'd been learning separately: navigation, Blueprint logic and sound. It ended up being one of the parts that contributed most to how tense the game felt.

Game systems design

Tutorial and difficulty progression

Once the maze and the statues were working together, I needed a reason to keep playing after escaping once. I went with an arcade-style progression that used the same graveyard and increased the difficulty each time you got through it.

The first level has no statues. It gives the player a chance to walk around, learn the space and work out that they're looking for three keys. On the second level, two statues become active. Each level after that adds another two until there are ten, and once the player gets through a level with all ten, the game increases their movement speed.

That gave me a way to build on the same basic task without making a new level for every round. Finding three keys gets more difficult when there are more statues to keep track of, and increasing their speed adds pressure once they're all in play.

Release and support

Packaging, launch and the first patch

With the graveyard working, I could finally see a version of the game I might be able to finish. My partner suggested releasing a horror game for Halloween, and that felt like a deadline that fit. I had about two weeks, so I started trying to get everything ready to package and put out.

Then packaging became its own problem. The project kept crashing, and I ended up going through Horror Engine and removing the parts I wasn't using. There were a lot of features I'd inherited when I picked that template, and by this point I had a small game that needed very few of them.

I spent hours deleting things and trying to make sure I wasn't breaking references somewhere else. It was tedious, and I was worried about pulling out something the game still needed, but I kept working through it until I had a project that would package. That shortcut from the start had caught up with me at the point where I had the least time to deal with it.

I missed Halloween by a day. NightShift went up on Itch.io on November 1, 2024.

Publishing the alpha and player response

Hitting publish was a mix of relief and nerves. I'd spent months thinking about this game and getting stuck on it, and now people could download it and play it. It was my first solo game release, so even getting to that point meant a lot to me.

I released it as NightShift Alpha, a free standalone experience built around finding three keys and escaping the graveyard. The museum and time-loop story were gone, but the interaction that had made me open Unreal in the first place was still there, now with a game around it.

The reception was modest. People downloaded it and added it to their collections, and someone chose to pay for it even though it was free. That was exciting to see after spending so much time working on it by myself. I was also proud of the atmosphere. The rain, the graveyard, the music and the minimal HUD had come together in a way I was happy to have people experience.

Fixing the launch bug in version 0.0.2

The launch version had a bug where the player's controls stopped working after Level 3. So a few days after getting the game out, I was working on a patch to fix it.

Version 0.0.2 fixed that input problem, improved the lighting and visuals, added text for the level transitions and refined the difficulty scaling. It felt good to be able to go back and improve the version people were playing. There were still things I would have liked to do, but fixing a problem that could stop someone from continuing was the immediate priority.

Lessons learned

What I learned from making NightShift

NightShift was where I started having to solve problems beyond the tutorials I'd been following. I could look up how to do a particular thing, but I still had to work out how it belonged in this game and what to do when it didn't behave the way I expected. The navigation, the heartbeat and even getting the project to package all gave me different versions of that problem.

The museum is probably the clearest example of what I had to learn about scope. I liked the idea, and I kept assuming I could figure it out if I spent more time on the layout. Meanwhile, I was making the project larger before I had a working game. Letting go of that setting was difficult after spending so much time thinking about it, but once I had the graveyard maze, I could make decisions and see them working together.

That was also what I enjoyed about the project once it started moving again. The paths gave the statues somewhere to navigate, the key locations gave the player somewhere to go, and the audio reacted to what was happening between them. The pieces I'd been working on started making sense in the same space. I could play through it and see what needed work next.

I'm still pretty proud of the full experience it creates. The rain, the sporadic thunder, the eerie music and the statues work well together, and it can be stressful to play even when I know how it's set up. There's plenty I would change, but I can still see the feeling I was trying to make when I started.

Finishing it mattered too. There were months when I was overwhelmed and didn't know how to move it forward, so having something people could download felt like a big step. It gave me experience of taking a project from an idea through all the less exciting parts of getting it out, including cutting things I liked and fixing problems after release.

Future improvements

What I would change

If I made NightShift again, I'd start with a clean project. Since finishing it, I've completed an Unreal Engine fellowship and have a much better understanding of how I'd put the game together. Horror Engine helped me get an early test running, but I spent a lot of time dealing with things I never needed. I'd rather build the smaller set of features the game uses from the start.

I'd also revisit the statue AI. The current Blueprint logic works, but it's messy and could be organised much better. I'd want to explore behaviour trees and state machines, with different states for things like patrolling, investigating sounds and hunting the player. Those would need to be tested with the observation mechanic to see what they add to the game.

Another thing I'd be interested in trying is a stamina system. Right now you can run indefinitely. Having to think about when to run and when to save that energy could change the decisions you make while moving through the graveyard, though I'd want to see how it felt alongside the statues before committing to it.

Performance and game balance

The game is fairly unoptimised and has frame-rate issues. I would want to spend time investigating those before deciding what to rebuild. There's work happening on Event Tick, including repeated casts, that I'd review, but I haven't established which parts are causing the slowdowns.

I'd also go back to the introduction and the difficulty balance. The first level feels boring without the statues, even though I put it there to help people learn the map. I'd want to figure out how to teach the goal while giving people an earlier taste of what the rest of the game is going to feel like.

Accessibility

There are basic things missing, like a way to lower the volume or mute the audio. The game also depends a lot on being able to see the statues and hear the warning cues, so accessibility would need more thought from the beginning. I didn't give that enough attention when I was making this version.

I don't expect to return to NightShift in the near future, but I do think I could rebuild the alpha much faster with what I know now. I can see where I made things difficult for myself, what I'd want to keep and where I'd start differently. The temptation to remake it is there. For now, it's useful to have this version to look back on and see how much I've learned since making it.
